<section id="tldr" class="tldr">
<span class="lbl">Take-home messages</span>
<ul>
<li><strong>When a condition is rare, most alerts are false.</strong> The share of alerts that are true is called the positive predictive value, and it depends on prevalence, the share of tested patients who have the condition. When 1 in 100 patients has the condition, only about one alert in six is true, even from a tool with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity.</li>
<li><strong>Work in counts of patients.</strong> Turn a tool's reported accuracy and the local rate of the condition into counts for 10,000 hypothetical patients, and the share of true alerts becomes a simple division. In one study, physicians estimated this share correctly in 46% of cases when the numbers were given as counts and in 10% when they were given as percentages.</li>
<li><strong>A study can report a higher share of true alerts than you will see.</strong> Some studies test a tool on patients in whom the condition is far more common than on a real ward, so a larger share of the alerts are true. Prefer results from consecutive patients in a setting like yours (every eligible patient, in order of arrival), or recalculate the share at your ward's rate of the condition.</li>
<li><strong>Count what the alerts cost.</strong> Before a tool goes live, estimate how many alerts the team must review to find one true case and what each review involves. When most alerts are false, clinicians may learn to override them by habit, and a true alert can then be overridden too.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="purpose">

## <span class="n">01</span>What a clinician needs to know when an alert fires

When an AI tool flags a patient, the clinician needs to know how likely it is that this patient has the condition, because the next step depends on that probability. This probability is called the positive predictive value (PPV, or precision in machine learning), and it equals the share of alerts that are true. Papers on AI tools often report two other measures first. Sensitivity (recall in machine learning) is the share of patients with the condition whom the tool flags. Specificity is the share of patients without the condition whom the tool leaves unflagged.

Suppose that 1 in 100 inpatients will have a serious adverse drug reaction, and that an AI tool flags those at high risk with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity. When the tool flags a patient, what is the probability that this patient will have the reaction?

The answer is about 16%, because the prevalence is only 1 in 100. Prevalence is the proportion of tested patients who have the condition. Sensitivity is measured only in patients with the condition and specificity only in patients without it, so in principle neither changes with prevalence. The PPV draws on both groups, so it does change with prevalence.

</section>

<section id="count">

## <span class="n">02</span>Counting patients instead of percentages

The PPV is easiest to compute, and hardest to get wrong, when the percentages are turned into counts of patients. Of 10,000 inpatients at this prevalence, 100 will have the reaction and 9,900 will not. With a sensitivity of 95%, the tool flags 95 of the 100 and misses 5. Because the specificity is 95%, it also flags 5% of the 9,900 who will not react, which is 495 patients. Of the 590 alerts, 95 are true. The PPV is therefore about 16%, and five of every six alerts are false (Figures 1 and 2).

The four boxes in Figure 1 form a 2x2 table, which engineers call a confusion matrix. The negative predictive value (NPV) is the share of unflagged patients who do not have the condition, and the same table gives it as 9,405 of 9,410, more than 99.9%.

Two surveys taken 35 years apart show how often physicians get this kind of question wrong. Both described a disease with a prevalence of 1 in 1,000 and a test with a false positive rate of 5% (a specificity of 95%). Respondents were asked for the chance that a person with a positive result has the disease. Assuming that the test detects every case (a sensitivity of 100%), the correct answer is about 2%. In 1978, [Casscells and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197811022991808) put the question to 60 medical students, house officers (residents) and attending physicians at Harvard Medical School, and 11 answered correctly. In 2013, [Manrai and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.1059) asked the same question of 61 physicians and medical students at a Boston-area hospital, and 14 answered correctly. The difference was not statistically significant, and in both surveys the most common answer was 95%.

Manrai's team observed that respondents often knew how to compute a PPV but accounted for prevalence incorrectly. One attending cardiologist wrote that "PPV does not depend on prevalence." The same error can occur with AI tools when a high sensitivity and specificity are read as a high chance that an alert is right.

The counts in Figures 1 and 2 are called natural frequencies, and two experiments show that they make this reasoning easier. In 1995, [Gigerenzer and Hoffrage](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684) gave 15 problems of this kind to 60 university students who were not familiar with Bayes' theorem, the formula behind the PPV calculation. When the problems gave the numbers as percentages, 16% of the answers used the correct reasoning. When they gave the same numbers as counts, 46% did. In 1998, [Hoffrage and Gigerenzer](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001888-199805000-00024) gave four diagnostic problems to 48 physicians in Munich and Düsseldorf. The physicians estimated the PPV correctly in 10% of cases when the numbers were probabilities and in 46% when they were natural frequencies (Figure 3). For this reason, I believe that anyone who evaluates an AI tool should convert its reported sensitivity and specificity into counts of patients at the local prevalence.

## <span class="n">03</span>Same tool, different ward

Because prevalence enters the calculation, the PPV is a property of the tool and the population together. On a high-risk ward, 1 in 10 patients will react, which is 1,000 of every 10,000. The tool flags 950 of them, and it also flags 450 of the 9,000 who will not react. The PPV is now 950 of 1,400 alerts, about 68%, although the tool has not changed (Figure 4).

Clinicians already reason this way with pretest probability, which is the probability of the condition before the test result is known. For suspected pulmonary embolism (a clot in the pulmonary arteries), the [American College of Physicians advises](https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-1772) clinicians to estimate the pretest probability first. When it is high, they should skip the D-dimer, a blood test for clots, and go straight to imaging with CT pulmonary angiography. I read this advice as the same arithmetic applied to a negative result. At a high pretest probability, the NPV of a normal D-dimer is too low to rule out the clot safely. For an AI tool, the pretest probability is the prevalence of the condition on the ward where the tool runs.

## <span class="n">04</span>Why a paper may report a better PPV than you will see

A paper reports a PPV from its test set, which is the group of patients in whom the study measured the tool's accuracy. That PPV therefore reflects the prevalence in the test set, which may differ from the prevalence on your ward. [Kim and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3348/kjr.2019.0025) reviewed 516 studies of AI for the diagnostic analysis of medical images, published from January to mid-August 2018. They distinguished two ways to build a test set. In a diagnostic cohort design, the team defines the clinical setting and eligibility criteria and then enrolls patients consecutively or at random. In a diagnostic case-control design, the team collects patients with and without the disease separately, sometimes from whatever data are at hand. Kim's team also recorded whether each study performed an external validation, which tests a model on data collected separately from the data used to build it. Only 5 of the 516 studies performed an external validation with a cohort design.

Kim's team names two problems with the case-control design. The first is unnatural prevalence. Because the researchers choose how many patients with the disease to include, they can enrich a test set with positive cases. Its PPV then reflects their mix of patients rather than the mix on your ward. The second is spectrum bias. The spectrum is the range of patients in whom a test is used, from mild to severe disease, including people without the disease whose findings mimic it. When a test set holds only clear-cut cases and clearly healthy controls, the tool looks better than it will on a real ward.

[Voter and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacr.2021.03.005) at the University of Wisconsin evaluated a commercial AI tool that flags intracranial hemorrhage (bleeding inside the skull) on non-contrast head CT. The tool had [510(k) clearance](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k) from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). This clearance requires a manufacturer to show that its device is as safe and effective as one already on the market. In the data reported for the clearance, about half of 198 scans had a bleed, and the Wisconsin authors inferred a PPV of 91.7% from the reported sensitivity and specificity. The Wisconsin team then tested the tool on 3,605 consecutive head CTs in adults from two emergency departments in the second half of 2019. Of these scans, 9.7% had a bleed. Of the 396 alerts that the tool raised, 74 were false, so the PPV was 81.3% (Figure 6).

Specificity was higher in Wisconsin (97.7% versus 92.3%), yet the PPV fell. Prevalence fell from 47.5% to 9.7%, which is enough to explain this (Figure 6). If Wisconsin's own sensitivity and specificity are applied at the prevalence of the clearance data, the PPV is about 97%. The authors are more cautious and write that the lower PPV "could be explained in part" by the lower prevalence. They also suggest that study design may explain much of the difference from earlier reports, since many earlier studies were small or enriched with bleeds.

To me, 81% is still a PPV that a radiologist can work with, but a department should plan around a PPV measured in consecutive patients like its own. When a paper reports results only from an enriched test set, recompute the PPV from its sensitivity and specificity at your local prevalence. Spectrum bias can still make that estimate optimistic, so confirm it in a silent deployment before you rely on it. In a silent deployment, the tool runs on your own patients, but clinicians do not see its alerts.

## <span class="n">05</span>What each alert costs the team

The first cost is the number of alerts that the team must answer for each true case. [Romero-Brufau and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-015-0999-1) proposed a measure of this cost, the number needed to evaluate (NNE), for early warning scores, which identify ward patients whose condition is getting worse. The NNE is the number of flagged patients whom clinicians must assess to find one true case, and it equals 1 divided by the PPV. In the worked example, the 590 alerts find 95 patients, an NNE of about 6. On the high-risk ward, the NNE is about 1.5.

The [Epic Sepsis Model](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.2626) shows the same burden in real patients. This proprietary model was developed by Epic, a vendor of electronic health records, and it was implemented at hundreds of US hospitals. In an external validation at Michigan Medicine, sepsis occurred in about 7% of 38,455 hospitalizations. The model gives each patient a score and alerts when the score passes a cut-off. At the cut-off that the authors chose, the model alerted on 18% of all hospitalizations, and its PPV was 12% (Figure 7). The authors also estimated the workload with only one alert per patient, issued the first time the score passed the cut-off. Even then, clinicians would need to evaluate 8 patients to find one who went on to develop sepsis, and repeated alerts for the same patient would add to that workload. This model also had a problem with its label, the answer that a model is trained to reproduce. [The previous post](/blog/proxy-surrogate-endpoint/#timing) explains that problem.

The second cost is what the team must do after each alert. Romero-Brufau and colleagues note that a PPV below 50% is generally unacceptable for a classic diagnostic test. For an early warning score, a PPV of 50% means that clinicians briefly assess two patients at the bedside for each true case. That burden may be acceptable when the predicted outcome is severe and often fatal. In the same way, a PPV of 16% may be acceptable if each alert costs a pharmacist a minute with the medication list. It is a different decision if each alert stops a drug that the patient needs.

False alerts also cost attention. In a [review of 17 studies](https://doi.org/10.1197/jamia.M1809), van der Sijs and colleagues found that clinicians override drug safety alerts in 49% to 96% of cases. They note that overriding may often be justified. They also list low specificity among the conditions that produce errors. When most alerts are false, clinicians can develop a habit of overriding them, which is called alert fatigue. I believe its main risk is that clinicians override a true alert as quickly as the false ones before it.

I recommend planning the alert burden before the tool goes live. At your local prevalence, estimate how many alerts the tool will raise each day and how many it takes to find one true case. Both numbers depend on the cut-off. Choose it together with the team that will answer the alerts, and agree in advance on what they will do after each one.

## <span class="n">06</span>Five questions to ask about any alerting tool

<ol class="num-list">
<li><strong>What is the prevalence in my setting?</strong> Among the patients whom the tool will see, at the moment when it runs.<span class="an">Pretest probability, for software</span></li>
<li><strong>What are the PPV and NPV at that prevalence?</strong> If the paper gives only sensitivity and specificity, work out the counts for 10,000 hypothetical patients yourself.<span class="an">A 2x2 table</span></li>
<li><strong>How many alerts per true case, and per day?</strong> This is the workload that the team will feel.<span class="an">Number needed to evaluate</span></li>
<li><strong>What happens after each alert?</strong> Who acts, and what a false alert costs the patient.<span class="an">A minute's review or a stopped drug</span></li>
<li><strong>Was the test set enriched?</strong> What share of the test patients had the condition, and were they enrolled consecutively?<span class="an">Case-control or cohort design</span></li>
</ol>
